Powers of Attorney vs. Guardianship: Why the Difference Matters

Both tools allow someone else to make decisions on your behalf. But one you choose proactively — and the other a court chooses for you.

If someone becomes unable to manage their own affairs — from a stroke, dementia, an accident, or another cause — someone else has to step in. The legal mechanism for that step-in is either a power of attorney that the person established in advance, or a court-appointed guardian or conservator if they didn't.

The difference between the two paths is enormous, and it's why every adult should have powers of attorney in place.

Powers of attorney: you choose, in advance

A durable power of attorney lets you name a person (your "agent") to make financial decisions for you if you can't. A patient advocate designation does the same for healthcare decisions. You choose who. You define the scope. You can revoke or update at any time while you're competent.

Guardianship: the court chooses, after the fact

Without powers of attorney, when capacity is lost, the family has to petition the probate court for a guardian (for personal decisions) or a conservator (for financial matters). The court holds hearings, may appoint a guardian ad litem to investigate, and decides who is appointed — which may or may not be the family member you would have chosen.

The practical differences

Guardianship is slow (often months from filing to appointment), expensive (court filings, attorney fees, sometimes a bond), public (everything goes on the court record), and ongoing (annual accountings, court reporting requirements). Powers of attorney avoid all of that.

What to put in place

A complete plan typically includes: a durable financial power of attorney, a patient advocate designation, HIPAA authorizations, and clear successor designations in case your first-choice agent is unavailable.

Get them done

Powers of attorney are inexpensive to prepare and they expire only on your death (or when you revoke them). If you don't have them in place, this is the single highest-leverage estate planning step you can take today. Read more about powers of attorney →
